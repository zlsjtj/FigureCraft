# DEMO — unedited rainwater diversion paragraphs

A small building has two rainwater tanks that supply separate non-potable loads. Three roof drainage branches feed them. The west roof drains only to the west tank and the service roof drains only to the east tank. A single diverter on the central roof branch divides that branch's flow between the two tanks. This central branch is one physical supply, not two independent supplies. Our initial operating note treated the total free volume as sufficient information for choosing the split. The constructed event below retains the separate tanks and their individual overflow outlets so that this assumption can be examined.

The four-minute event has constant source flows of 20, 30, and 10 L/min from the west, central, and service roofs, respectively. The west tank starts at 180 L with a 200 L capacity, and the east tank starts at 250 L with a 300 L capacity. The loads continuously remove 18 L/min from the west tank and 24 L/min from the east tank. The loads are supplied throughout this example; neither tank becomes empty. There is no pump or balancing pipe between tanks. Water that spills from either tank is lost to a drain and cannot reach the other tank.

Let alpha be the fraction of the central roof flow sent west. The central flow sent east is therefore one minus alpha. A historical setting uses alpha equal to one third. A delayed setting retains that fraction for the first minute and then changes alpha to 0.10 at exactly 60 seconds. The operation note also includes a fixed setting of 0.10 selected before the event begins. Each of these schedules is evaluated independently from the same initial volumes. There is no input forecast error or actuation uncertainty in the constructed calculation, so a comparison cannot establish whether a delayed schedule is preferable when forecasts are wrong.

We generated one-minute balance records, with source inflow and load withdrawal acting continuously during each interval. Overflow removes the excess immediately when a tank reaches capacity. The records distinguish the unconstrained net volume change from the volume actually stored. A control change does not move water that was stored during the preceding minute. Flow fractions sum to one at the diverter, but the inflows to the two tanks need not be equal after the setting changes. Values in liters and liters per minute should not be combined without the interval duration.

The three schedules produce different cumulative spill totals, although they receive the same 240 L of runoff and deliver the same 168 L to the loads. The interval file includes tank-specific spill and ending volume for all schedules. The apparent advantage of changing the divider after one minute should be judged alongside the inexpensive fixed alternative in the same record. Any interpretation also needs to respect the combined storage bound and the restriction against moving water between tanks. No storm distribution, tank-level sensor, control optimization, or physical valve has been tested. These numbers are a transparent DEMO balance exercise and cannot establish field savings, flood protection, or a new control method.
